WIN IF YOU CAN is an AI hackathon built around a simple engineering idea: a familiar 
problem can become a very different challenge when the obvious technical route is 
unavailable. Conventional hackathons often reward speed in selecting a known model, 
library or architecture. This competition deliberately introduces a BAN after the problem is 
revealed. The BAN is not there to make the task arbitrary; it is there to force teams to 
understand the capability they need rather than merely reproduce a familiar recipe. The 
central skill is therefore not memorising the newest AI technique. It is reasoning from the 
requirement to a workable system, even when the first idea has been removed. 
 
Every challenge can be understood as a chain of six elements: FIELD ΓåÆ PROBLEM ΓåÆ 
REQUIRED OUTCOME ΓåÆ BAN ΓåÆ ALTERNATIVE SOLUTION ΓåÆ VALIDATION. The field 
establishes the technical territory, such as Computer Vision, Machine Learning, Deep 
Learning, NLP, LLM or Optimization. The problem describes the real-world situation that 
needs to be addressed. The required outcome defines what the system must actually produce, 
while the BAN identifies a route, component or capability that cannot be used under the 
challenge rules. The team must then discover an alternative route that still satisfies the 
requirement and produce evidence that the route works. 
 
Treat the BAN as a design constraint rather than an inconvenience. A constraint changes the 
search space, and that change can reveal engineering choices that would otherwise be 
ignored. If a team immediately starts coding around a favourite framework, it may never 
understand which part of that framework is actually responsible for the required capability. A 
stronger approach is to ask: What does the banned approach normally provide? Which 
capability is essential to the outcome? 
Can that capability be produced by another algorithm, representation, data transformation, 
mathematical method, retrieval mechanism, heuristic or hybrid architecture? This shift turns 
the BAN into a structured reasoning exercise. 
The strongest teams will not be the teams that produce the most complicated diagram. They 
will be teams that can explain why each important component exists, what alternatives were 
considered, what evidence supports the chosen approach and where the system can fail. Start 
with the requirement, not the tool. Build a small 
end-to-end system before polishing individual components. Measure something meaningful 
before claiming success. Keep a record of experiments so that a failed idea becomes 
evidence rather than lost time. Above all, make sure the final system is something the team 
can defend honestly. 
 
CHALLENGE MAP 
Use the following mental model throughout the event: INPUT ΓåÆ PROCESS ΓåÆ DECISION 
ΓåÆ OUTPUT ΓåÆ MEASUREMENT. The input may be text, images, numerical 
observations, constraints or multiple modalities. The process transforms those inputs into 
useful representations or candidate solutions. The decision stage applies a model, rule, 
search procedure or optimization objective. The output is the concrete result the evaluator 
cares about. Measurement closes the loop by showing whether the result is useful, reliable 
and compliant. When a team feels stuck, return to this chain and identify which stage is 
unclear.

TEAM FORMATION  Teams consist of 2ΓÇô4 students, making collaboration important from the first minute. 
Complementary skills can help: one person may be stronger in data and 
experimentation, another in software engineering, another in model design, and another in 
communication or demonstration. However, role distribution should not create isolated 
knowledge. Every member should understand the problem statement, the architecture, the 
major experiments and the reason the final approach complies with the BAN. A useful 
practice is to nominate a lead for each responsibility while keeping short shared checkpoints 
where the whole team reviews decisions. 
CARD DRAW 
The team leader randomly draws one of six fields: Computer Vision, Machine 
Learning, Deep Learning, NLP, LLM or Optimization. The card establishes the 
technical domain, but it does not reveal the complete challenge. That distinction matters. 
Participants should resist preparing a solution before the challenge is known. Once the 
field is drawn, use it as a vocabulary for asking questions: what kinds of inputs are 
common here, what outputs are normally required, what baseline techniques exist, and what 
alternative approaches could provide similar capabilities? The card narrows the language of 
the problem without giving away the answer.

After the draw, the team receives a real-world problem, the required outcome and a BAN. 
Read all three together. A problem statement describes context, but the required outcome 
tells you what must be demonstrated. The BAN then removes one route from the design 
space. If a team reads only the problem and starts coding, it can spend most of the round 
building something that later turns out to violate a constraint or solve the wrong output. 
Spend the first minutes marking nouns, verbs, measurable outcomes, constraints,

THE SIX AI FIELDS 
03 / KNOW THE LANGUAGE OF YOUR CARD 
COMPUTER VISION Computer Vision deals with extracting useful information from images and video. The required 
output determines the strategy: detection identifies objects or regions, 
recognition assigns meaning, segmentation separates pixels or regions, and tracking follows 
entities across time. A visual problem is therefore not automatically a 
deep-learning problem. Teams should first specify what information must be extracted and 
how accurate that extraction needs to be. Classical image processing, geometric reasoning, 
feature-based methods and learned representations can occupy different places in the same 
pipeline when the constraint permits them. 
MACHINE 
LEARNING 
Machine Learning focuses on learning patterns from data so a system can classify, predict, 
rank, estimate or detect anomalies. The important engineering decisions include how 
examples are represented, how training and evaluation data are separated, which baseline 
establishes a reference point, and whether the learned relationship generalizes beyond the 
examples used during development. 
Classification, regression, clustering and anomaly detection have different objectives 
and 
therefore require different measurements. A model that performs well on familiar samples 
can still be weak if its errors change when the input distribution changes. 
 
DEEP LEARNING 
Deep Learning uses neural networks to learn layered representations from data. 
Convolutional architectures have historically been effective for many spatial signals, 
while transformer architectures are widely used for sequences and multimodal tasks. The 
attraction of deep models is their ability to learn complex representations, but complexity 
does not automatically equal quality. Teams still need suitable data, a clear objective, 
appropriate evaluation and enough compute to iterate. Under a BAN, the key question is not 
whether a deep model is fashionable; it is which capability the model contributes and what 
other mechanism could provide that capability. 
NLP 
Natural Language Processing concerns how systems represent and work with human 
language. Practical tasks can involve intent detection, entity extraction, 
classification, summarization, retrieval, semantic matching or structured information 
extraction. Language is context-sensitive, so a system should be tested on ambiguity, 
unusual phrasing and cases where a keyword-based rule can mislead. A strong NLP design 
defines the desired output precisely and chooses representations and evaluation criteria 
around that output rather than assuming that general language understanding is required for 
every task. 
LLM 
Large Language Models generate or transform language using learned 
representations of context. They can support reasoning workflows, drafting, 
extraction, transformation and structured outputs, but they can also produce confident errors. 
Effective use therefore requires explicit prompts or schemas, constrained outputs where 
appropriate, verification steps and evaluation against representative examples. If an LLM or a 
related component is unavailable because of the BAN, identify the underlying capability you 
needΓÇösuch as semantic matching or structured extractionΓÇöand investigate alternative 
representations, retrieval, rules or smaller models that can provide that capability. 
OPTIMIZATION 
Optimization formalizes a goal and searches for a solution under constraints. Typical tasks 
include scheduling, routing, allocation, packing and resource planning. A 
feasible solution merely satisfies the constraints; a better solution also improves an objective 
such as time, cost, distance or resource use. Teams should define the decision variables, 
objective function and constraints before selecting a solver or heuristic. Exact methods, 
greedy approaches, local search, metaheuristics and hybrid strategies can be appropriate in 
different problem sizes. The field gives you the language of the challenge; your team must 
still discover the solution.

FIRST: READ 
When the challenge arrives, resist the urge to open the editor immediately. Read the problem, 
required outcome, BAN and any stated constraints as one system. Mark the 
exact nouns that describe inputs and outputs, the verbs that describe required actions, and the 
conditions that determine success. Rewrite ambiguous language into questions. For example: 
What is given? What must be produced? What is the unit of evaluation? What information 
can the system legitimately use? Which part of the obvious approach is forbidden? This first 
pass prevents hours of implementation aimed at the wrong target.

Rewrite the challenge in your own technical language. Convert the real-world story into an 
input, transformation, decision, output and measurement pipeline. Then state the problem in 
one sentence that a technical teammate could implement. If the team cannot agree on that 
sentence, the problem is not yet understood. Next, separate requirements from assumptions. 
A requirement comes from the challenge; an assumption is something the team has 
introduced. Keeping those categories separate makes later defence much easier because 
judges can ask which decisions were mandated and which were engineering choices. 
 
Success must be measurable. Decide what a useful output looks like before choosing the final 
architecture. Depending on the task, that might mean correctness of a classification, quality 
of extracted fields, retrieval relevance, error magnitude, latency, constraint satisfaction, route 
cost or another problem-specific measure. 
Establish a simple baseline as early as possible. A baseline does not need to be sophisticated; 
it exists so the team can say whether the new approach is actually improving the outcome 
rather than merely producing an interesting demonstration. 
Deliberately write down the approach the team would normally use if there were no BAN. 
This is valuable because it reveals the capability that the BAN is targeting. Then remove that 
route from the design. Do not quietly recreate it through a wrapper, API, pretrained 
component or hidden dependency. Instead, ask what the banned route was doing for you. 
Was it extracting features, producing embeddings, searching a space, interpreting language, 
recognizing patterns or solving an optimization subproblem? Once the capability is explicit, 
the team can search for alternatives without being trapped by the name of the banned 
technology. 
The first hour should produce clarity, not polish. Start with a shared reading, then a technical 
restatement, a success metric and a list of constraints. Spend focused time generating several 
candidate alternatives rather than falling in love with the first 
one. Run the cheapest 
experiment that can eliminate a weak candidate. By the end of the first hour, aim to have a 
baseline, a candidate architecture, a test plan and a clear statement of BAN compliance. A 
small working pipeline is more valuable than a large unfinished system because it gives the 
team something measurable to improve.
